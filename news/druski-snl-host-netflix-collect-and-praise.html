Druski Hosts SNL This Saturday, Then Brings Collect and Praise to Netflix in December

By Maccarthy Albin

November 2, 2026 · 4:00 PM GMT

The comedian once heard no from Netflix, Amazon and FX. This week he hosts Saturday Night Live, and next month Netflix releases his scripted series.

Few comedians have gone from internet skits to the front door of major Hollywood institutions as quickly as Druski. On Saturday, Nov. 7, the Baltimore-born creator makes his “Saturday Night Live” hosting debut. A few weeks later, Netflix premieres Collect and Praise, his new scripted comedy series.

  • Druski hosts “Saturday Night Live” on Nov. 7 with The Strokes as musical guest
  • His scripted Netflix comedy series Collect and Praise debuts in December
  • Netflix, Amazon and FX previously passed on his pitches
  • Netflix also began production on the comedy film 72 Hours with Kevin Hart and Kai Cenat

A first for “SNL”

NBC announced the lineup for the 52nd season of “Saturday Night Live” in late October, and Druski’s name was one of the headline items. He will host the episode on Nov. 7 with The Strokes as musical guest, sharing a hosting slate with actor Inde Navarrette and comedian John Mulaney. Puck reported that Druski will be the first online creator to headline an episode of the NBC sketch institution, a distinction that has already set off a round of commentary about what it means for both sides of the arrangement.

For “SNL,” the booking is a bet on an audience that does not necessarily wait for Saturday at 11:30 p.m. Druski’s sketches, which tend to run a minute or two and spread through social feeds within hours, have trained a generation of viewers to expect comedy in short, shareable bursts. Whether that instinct translates to a ninety-minute live show with a cast, a rehearsal schedule and a single take is the open question. Live sketch comedy rewards economy and character work, and Druski has built a career on exactly those skills, but he has done it on his own timeline, with his own editing room.

The critics have framed the moment in two competing ways. One camp sees “SNL” as the thing that finally legitimizes a performer long dismissed as a mere “online content creator.” The other camp, including writers at The A.V. Club, has pointed out that a scripted Netflix series arriving in December complicates that premise. If a major streamer is already building a show around his work, the argument goes, the creator label stopped describing him some time ago.

Either way, the timing is hard to overlook. In the space of a few weeks, Druski moves from one of television’s most storied stages to one of the world’s largest streaming platforms, and both of those doors opened for him after years of building an audience on his own.

What Collect and Praise is

The Netflix project at the center of this story is Collect and Praise, a scripted comedy series built around Druski’s megachurch pastor character. The figure is one of the best-known recurring bits in his catalog: a flamboyant, prosperity-minded preacher whose sermons skewer the commercial side of big-ticket religious spectacle. The character has been a fixture of his online work for years, and it has also shown up in his acting credits, including the 2023 film Praise This, in which he appears as Aaron, according to listings.

The series was originally developed with his YouTube channel in mind. EURweb reported that Netflix picked up the project after that, which means a concept designed for a creator’s own channel has instead become a streaming original. The streamer has described it as a new scripted comedy series that debuts in December. A specific premiere date and a full cast list had not been widely reported at the time of writing, so those details remain to be confirmed.

That detail matters, because it reverses the usual direction of travel. Traditionally, a comedian pitched an idea to a network, waited for a verdict and, if the answer was yes, made it on someone else’s terms. In this case, the idea existed first, with a built-in audience and a track record, and the platform came to it. That is a much stronger bargaining position, and it is part of why industry watchers have paid close attention to the deal.

For Netflix, the appeal is not hard to understand. A character with a ready-made fan base lowers the marketing burden, and a creator who has already shown he can write, produce and star in his own material lowers the production risk. For Druski, the series offers something his online work cannot: the scale, budget and global distribution of a major streaming release.

The years of “no”

None of this was inevitable, and Druski has been unusually candid about how long it took. In a September interview on “The Diary of a CEO” with host Steven Bartlett, he said that Netflix, Amazon and FX were among the companies that repeatedly passed on his pitches. According to coverage of the conversation, FX turned down a proposed show, Amazon passed on a movie, and Netflix rejected multiple projects over the years. In a separate profile in Essence, he said he had also tried to interest Hulu, and that the response to his short-form ideas was a long string of refusals.

The same coverage describes a harder side of the story. Druski told Bartlett about a dangerous night involving an armed robbery, which he has connected to his decision to commit fully to comedy. He has described the incident as a turning point, the moment he decided to become a comedian or fail in the attempt. The detail is part of why his rise has resonated beyond the usual entertainment-industry narrative: the persistence was not a branding exercise. It grew out of a sense that he had no better option.

He has also spoken about the people who helped along the way. Headlines about the Bartlett interview note a life-changing co-sign from Drake, a kind of endorsement that carries weight in a business where attention is currency. Still, the throughline in everything he has said publicly is the same: the major gatekeepers were not interested, and he stopped waiting for them.

Looking back, he has acknowledged in interviews that his early approach may have been premature. In remarks reported by AfroTech, he said that his team tried too early to push their work to the biggest companies. The lesson, as he describes it, was to build more first and ask later.

Betting on himself

What followed was a strategy that has since become a model for other creators. Rather than keep pitching, Druski took money from brand deals and invested it in making his own shows. He put those productions on YouTube, where he controlled the pacing, the casting and the tone, and where his audience could vote with its attention.

The results were visible. His slate of self-produced projects included Coulda Been Love, a dating series that became a talking point online, and Coulda Been House, a follow-up built on the same ensemble energy. He also partnered with streamer Kai Cenat on a venture called Coulda Been Records. The projects blurred the line between a comedy sketch and a television format, and they showed that his audience would follow him across genres.

Money followed attention. AfroTech reported that Druski expanded his business portfolio with an equity stake in the delivery company Gopuff, a sign that his commercial ambitions extend well beyond comedy. Baller Alert framed the broader arc with a headline that captured the irony: he bet millions on himself after Hollywood said no, and now Netflix and Amazon want back in. In his telling, the rejection turned into a bidding war.

The self-funding approach also gave him leverage that few first-time series creators have. When he sat down with a streamer again, he was not asking for a chance. He was bringing finished work and a proven audience, and he could afford to walk away.

Netflix was already in his orbit

Although Collect and Praise is the headline project, it is not the first sign that Netflix and Druski were drifting toward each other. In May 2025, 3Music reported that the streamer had begun production on 72 Hours, a comedy film starring Kevin Hart alongside Druski and Kai Cenat. Saturday Night Live breakout Marcello Hernández was cast as the groom’s best man, giving the ensemble a mix of veteran stand-up, digital-native fame and sketch-comedy pedigree. A release date had not been announced when production began, so the film’s timing remains one to watch.

Earlier still, The Hollywood Reporter noted that Druski had an untitled show based on his airport skits in early development at Netflix. Those skits, which put his characters through the absurdities of air travel, are among his most widely shared work. The report described him as someone who was not waiting around for the next opportunity, which turned out to be an accurate read.

Hart has been generous in public about the new generation. Speaking on his “360” interview series, he called Druski and Cenat amazing, talented performers in a younger wave of comedy, while adding that they are far from the only ones. Coming from one of the most commercially successful stand-ups of the past two decades, and a frequent Netflix collaborator, the comment reads as a passing of the baton, delivered with a joke attached.

Beyond Netflix, Deadline reported in June that Druski joined Emma Stone in the baseball romantic comedy The Catch, a sign that traditional studios are also eager to put him on screen. Taken together, the projects suggest that his career is no longer a one-platform story.

A wider shift in who gets a streaming deal

Druski’s rise fits a pattern that streaming companies have been leaning into. Netflix has increasingly looked to personalities who already command large audiences online, rather than relying only on traditional development pipelines. Earlier this year, for example, the streamer launched The Pete Davidson Show, filmed largely from the comedian’s garage and released weekly, an approach the Webby Awards praised for bringing the intimacy of podcast culture to a global audience.

The logic is straightforward. A creator with millions of followers has already demonstrated demand, and the cost of producing a short, character-driven comedy is a fraction of a prestige drama. A streamer can test the format, measure the response and expand if it works. For the creators, the deals offer reach, resources and a measure of prestige that social platforms alone do not provide.

There is a trade-off, and Druski has hinted that he understands it. Moving from a channel he owns to a platform that owns the series means giving up some control, and giving up the ability to publish whatever he wants on whatever schedule he chooses. He has built his brand on speed and independence. How well that brand adapts to the more deliberate rhythms of a Netflix release will be part of the story in December.

Attention comes with scrutiny

A bigger platform also brings closer attention. Druski’s sketches have at times sparked intense online debate, including a widely discussed sketch earlier this year that featured him in whiteface and drew a fierce reaction from viewers. Other bits, such as a viral clip imagining Chris Rock in disguise, have circulated just as widely for lighter reasons. His comedy relies on exaggeration and character, and the audience for it is large and opinionated.

That dynamic will intensify as his work reaches television audiences who may not be familiar with his online catalog. Netflix has a long history of defending its comedians’ creative freedom, with co-CEO Ted Sarandos stating in the past that the company works to support talent even when some viewers find the material objectionable. How Druski navigates that terrain, with a network’s attention on him and a live “SNL” audience watching this week, may shape how broadly his comedy lands.

What comes next

The next few weeks will test the thesis that an internet-born comedian can carry traditional television. The “SNL” episode on Nov. 7 is the first test: a live broadcast, a global audience, and a hosting job that has humbled seasoned performers. If it goes well, it will reinforce the idea that his online style translates to the biggest stages in comedy.

Then comes December, when Collect and Praise arrives on Netflix. The series will answer a different set of questions. Can a single character sustain a full scripted season? Will fans of the YouTube sketches follow the pastor into a longer format? Can a show built for one platform thrive on another? Those answers will matter not only for Druski but for the creators watching to see how far the model can go.

For now, the story is one of reversal. The same streamer that once said no is preparing to release his series, and the television show that once seemed out of reach has booked him as its host. Druski has not changed his message much along the way. He kept making the work, and the industry caught up.
