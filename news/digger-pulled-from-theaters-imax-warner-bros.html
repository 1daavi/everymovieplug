Tom Cruise's Digger Is Being Pulled From Theaters as Warner Bros. Retreats

By Maccarthy Albin

October 15, 2026

The film is losing its IMAX screens and most regular ones after just two weekends in release.

Warner Bros. is pulling Digger from a large number of theaters. Showbiz411 reported on October 13 that the studio would remove the Tom Cruise film from most prominent locations after Wednesday, October 14, with a fuller retreat on Thursday, October 15.

A withdrawal of this speed is unusual for a major studio release featuring one of Hollywood's best-known stars, and it comes only two weekends after the film opened. The reports from several outlets paint a consistent picture of a film that has struggled to find an audience and of a studio reallocating its screens quickly.

Where the Film Is Disappearing

According to Showbiz411, the film is being removed entirely from places such as Times Square in Manhattan, and it is losing its IMAX screens and most regular screens. One Upper West Side screen will keep a VistaVision print because the studio installed projection hardware there. The report is based on theater schedules in Manhattan, downtown Chicago and the New York suburbs, and says the film was on 3,321 screens.

The details show that the pullback is concentrated in the most visible venues. Times Square locations carry symbolic weight, since they serve as a showcase for major releases and draw large numbers of tourists. A film removed from them entirely has effectively lost its public presence in the city's busiest entertainment district. The report relies on theater listings in a limited set of markets, so the picture elsewhere may differ, but the examples from Manhattan, Chicago and the New York suburbs point to a broad reduction rather than a handful of isolated cuts.

The VistaVision exception is an interesting footnote. VistaVision is a film format that runs 35mm stock horizontally through the camera to produce a larger image, and it has been revived in recent years by filmmakers who want a distinctive look. The studio installed projection hardware at the Upper West Side venue to show the film in that format, which explains why the screen will keep its print even as the rest of the run winds down.

IMAX Screens Pass to The Odyssey

The IMAX loss came a week earlier. Collider reported on October 11 that Digger, directed by Alejandro G. Iñárritu, was being pulled from IMAX venues after one week, with Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey returning to digital IMAX and IMAX 70mm screens.

Premium large-format screens are scarce and valuable, and exhibitors prefer to fill them with films that sell tickets at higher prices. The return of Nolan's film to those auditoriums indicates that the screens were redirected to a title with stronger demand. Losing those screens after a single week represents a significant setback, since premium auditoriums generate higher ticket prices and attract the most committed moviegoers.

A Timeline of the Pullback

The sequence of events has moved quickly. Collider's report on the IMAX removal came on October 11, Showbiz411 followed on October 13 with its account of the wider retreat, and the removals themselves were scheduled to take effect after Wednesday, October 14, with the fuller pullback on Thursday, October 15. That compressed schedule, spanning only a few days, shows how rapidly a studio can reassign screens once a film's performance is clear.

The Numbers Behind the Retreat

The numbers explain the retreat. The Daily Beast reports the film opened to $8 million domestic and $20 million worldwide, then reached $14.4 million domestic and $35.3 million worldwide after its second weekend. Deseret News says ticket sales fell 60% in the second weekend. The Daily Beast says Digger is on track to lose about $245 million and finish near $50 million worldwide, against a break-even point that Variety puts near $350 million and Warner Bros. puts at $300 million.

A drop of 60% between a first and second weekend is steep and indicates weak word of mouth or limited audience interest. Healthy films typically see smaller declines, and a fall of that size makes it difficult to build momentum through later weeks. The projected finish near $50 million worldwide would leave the film far short of either break-even estimate, and the difference between the two estimates, $300 million from the studio and $350 million from Variety, reflects different methods of calculating the point at which a film recovers its costs. All of these figures are projections and estimates reported by outlets, and final totals will depend on the film's remaining run and on international markets.

What the Film Cost to Make and Sell

Production costs were estimated at $160 million to $180 million, with about $100 million more for marketing, according to Variety as cited by Deseret News. For our earlier coverage of the film's box office, read our report on the Digger second-weekend loss.

Taken together, production and marketing add up to a substantial investment, in the range of $260 million to $280 million before theaters take their share of ticket sales. That scale of spending is why the break-even estimates are so high: studios typically need box office revenue well above a film's total cost to recover it, because exhibitors keep a significant portion of each ticket sold. Against that backdrop, a worldwide total near $50 million would represent a considerable shortfall.

A Difficult Weekend for the Fall Box Office

The Deseret News headline groups Digger with another release, The Social Reckoning, as the two flops that slowed the fall box office. The pairing suggests the problem extends beyond a single title and that audiences have been selective in the early weeks of the season. For theater owners, a stretch of weak openings makes decisions about which films to keep and which to replace more urgent, and it explains why a studio might move quickly once the numbers become clear.

What Happens Next

With the film leaving most major venues, its remaining life is likely to be in smaller theaters and, eventually, on home platforms. The figures cited here come from outlet projections rather than final studio statements. Director Alejandro G. Iñárritu, known for films such as Birdman and The Revenant, and star Tom Cruise, whose recent hits include Top Gun: Maverick, are both associated with box office and critical success, which makes the film's reception a notable departure from their records.
