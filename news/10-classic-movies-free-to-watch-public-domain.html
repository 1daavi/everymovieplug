10 Classic Movies You Can Watch for Free Because They're in the Public Domain

By Maccarthy Albin

October 19, 2026

From silent landmarks to a Hitchcockian thriller, these films cost nothing to watch legally in the U.S.

You do not need a subscription to watch some of the best films ever made. The ten movies below are in the public domain in the United States, which means they can be watched and shared legally, and copies are commonly offered free by archives and video sites. Each title has a card linking to its page on Every Movie Plug.

What “Public Domain” Really Means for Movies

A film in the public domain is no longer protected by copyright, so anyone may watch, share and show it without paying a license fee. Films reach that status in different ways. Some are old enough that their copyright terms have simply expired. Others lost protection because of paperwork: under earlier U.S. rules, many films needed a proper copyright notice on release and a renewal after an initial term, and works that missed one of those steps fell into the public domain.

The ten titles here show all three routes. The silent classics are old enough that their terms have run out, while films such as Night of the Living Dead, Charade and His Girl Friday are free because of missing notices or renewals. The mix gives a sense of how many types of cinema, from silent spectacle to screwball comedy to low-budget horror, are available at no cost.

1. Night of the Living Dead (1968)

George A. Romero's horror classic is public domain because its copyright notice was accidentally removed during a title change. The film was originally released under a different title, and when it was renamed for distribution the required notice did not carry over. Under the rules in force at the time, that omission meant the film lost its copyright protection almost immediately, which is why it has circulated freely for decades.

That quirk of paperwork helped the film reach an enormous audience. Made on a modest independent budget, it became one of the most influential horror films ever released, and its claustrophobic farmhouse setting and bleak tone shaped the zombie genre that followed.

2. Nosferatu (1922)

The silent vampire film about Count Orlok, whose copyright has expired. Directed by F. W. Murnau, it was an unauthorized adaptation of Dracula, and Bram Stoker's estate took legal action over it. A court ordered copies destroyed, yet prints survived and the film was eventually rediscovered and celebrated.

Max Schreck's rat-like, shadow-casting Orlok remains one of cinema's most recognizable monsters, and the film's story continues to inspire new versions, including Robert Eggers's 2024 remake. Watching the original is a useful way to see where so many later vampire images began.

3. Metropolis (1927)

Fritz Lang's sci-fi epic about a city divided between rich and poor. A film released in 1927 is old enough that its U.S. copyright term has run out, which is why it is widely available.

Its vision of towering skyscrapers above an underground working class has been borrowed by science fiction ever since. Because the film was cut heavily after its premiere and later partly reassembled, different editions run to different lengths, so it is worth choosing a version that notes how complete it is.

4. A Trip to the Moon (1902)

Georges Méliès's short about explorers fired to the Moon from a giant cannon. Running only around a quarter of an hour at most, it is one of the earliest science fiction films and a landmark of early special effects, built from painted sets, stage tricks and double exposures.

Its best-known image, a rocket lodged in the eye of the Moon's face, is among the most recognized in film history. The movie also appeared in Martin Scorsese's Hugo, which introduced it to a new generation of viewers.

5. The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920)

The German Expressionist horror landmark. Directed by Robert Wiene, it is known for sets of jagged, distorted shapes and painted shadows that reflect the unstable minds of its characters rather than the real world.

The story is told as a recollection, and its framing device became a model for later psychological thrillers. Conrad Veidt appears as the sleepwalker Cesare, and the film's look influenced horror and film noir for generations.

6. Charade (1963)

The Cary Grant and Audrey Hepburn thriller, in the public domain in the U.S. because it was released without a copyright notice, per the Internet Archive. Directed by Stanley Donen, it blends mystery, romance and comedy against a Parisian backdrop, with a score by Henry Mancini.

It is often described as the best Hitchcock film Hitchcock never made, a nod to its twisting plot and elegant suspense. Supporting roles from Walter Matthau, James Coburn and George Kennedy add menace and humor, and the central pairing of Grant and Hepburn is a major draw.

7. His Girl Friday (1940)

The screwball newspaper comedy, whose copyright was not renewed in 1968. Directed by Howard Hawks, it adapts the play The Front Page, with Cary Grant as a scheming editor and Rosalind Russell as the star reporter who is also his ex-wife.

Its signature feature is dialogue delivered at breakneck speed, with characters talking over one another in a way that feels startlingly modern. The pace is a large part of why the film is regularly cited among the finest American comedies.

8. Plan 9 from Outer Space (1957)

Ed Wood's cult sci-fi film, whose copyright was not renewed. It is frequently ranked among the worst films ever made, and that reputation has become part of its appeal, with audiences embracing its wobbly sets, awkward dialogue and unlikely plot.

Bela Lugosi, who died before the production was completed, appears in footage Wood shot earlier, which adds an unusual and slightly eerie layer to the viewing. The film is best enjoyed as a piece of cheerful, enthusiastic independent filmmaking.

9. The General (1926)

Buster Keaton's Civil War locomotive chase, a film old enough that its U.S. copyright has expired. Keaton, who stars and co-directed, performs his own stunts atop and around moving trains, and the film's pacing and physical precision remain impressive.

The movie was not a success on its release, but critics have since placed it among the greatest comedies ever made. Its use of real locomotives and large-scale action sequences gives it a scale that still looks remarkable.

10. The Kid (1921)

Charlie Chaplin's silent comedy about a tramp raising an abandoned boy. It was Chaplin's first full-length feature as director, and it blends slapstick with sentiment in a way that became central to his work.

Jackie Coogan, who plays the child, shares many of the film's best scenes with Chaplin, and the pair's chemistry carries the story. The film demonstrates how silent cinema could be both funny and emotionally direct.

Where to Start, Depending on Your Mood

With ten very different films on the list, it helps to pick by appetite. Horror fans have the most direct choices in Night of the Living Dead, Nosferatu and The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari, which together trace the genre from German Expressionism to American independent shock. Viewers who prefer laughs can turn to His Girl Friday, The General and The Kid, which span fast-talking sound comedy and two very different silent styles.

Anyone curious about science fiction can pair A Trip to the Moon with Metropolis to see how the genre looked at its beginning and as it matured a quarter of a century later. Charade suits viewers who want a polished, star-driven thriller, and Plan 9 from Outer Space is the pick for a night of cheerful bad-movie viewing. Short entries such as A Trip to the Moon are a good way to sample silent cinema before committing to a feature-length film.

Check the Edition Before You Press Play

Public domain status depends on the country and on the specific edition. Restored or re-edited versions of older films can still be protected, so watch a version that is offered free by its host. A film that is free in the United States may still be under copyright elsewhere, and a modern restoration, new soundtrack or added commentary can carry its own protection even when the underlying film does not.

The practical advice is simple: use reputable archives and sources that state a film's status, and avoid copies of unclear origin. Reasons for each film's status are drawn from Movies Found Online and the Internet Archive.
