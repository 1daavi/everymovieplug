10 Iconic Hollywood Movies Where the Villain Wins

By Maccarthy Albin

October 8, 2026

The hero usually prevails. These films decided that darkness gets the last word. Major spoilers ahead.

For decades, Hollywood followed an unwritten rule: no matter how bleak the second act gets, the hero prevails, justice is served, and evil is punished. Every so often, though, filmmakers break that rule and let the darkness win.

When the bad guy comes out on top, it leaves a mark. A standard thriller becomes an unforgettable gut punch. Here are ten films where evil walked away victorious.

The Rule Hollywood Once Wrote Into Its Own Rulebook

The “unwritten rule” was not always unwritten. From the mid-1930s until the late 1960s, American studios followed the Production Code, a self-censorship system that discouraged films from showing crime as profitable and expected wrongdoers to be punished by the end of the story. Directors who wanted to explore moral ambiguity had to hide it in subtext or tack on a closing scene in which the villain got what was coming to him.

That system gave way in 1968, when the industry adopted the ratings system still in use today. The result was a new kind of American movie. Filmmakers of the 1970s were free to end stories in defeat, and audiences who had grown skeptical of institutions were ready to watch them do it. The films below span half a century, and most of them arrived after that wall came down.

What Counts as a Win

Not every victory on this list looks the same. Some villains accomplish exactly what they set out to do. Others escape punishment, or leave the heroes broken, or are simply rewarded by a world that was never going to stop them. For the purposes of this list, a villain wins when the story ends with the villain’s central goal achieved or the hero’s central goal lost, and when the film does not offer a clear moral correction afterward. One entry is a looser case, and it is flagged as such.

The Masterminds: Villains Who Plan Their Victory

The most satisfying villain wins are often the most carefully designed. In these four films, the bad guy knows more than the audience, and the ending reveals that the story was theirs all along.

1. Se7en (1995)

The villain: John Doe (Kevin Spacey)
The victory: Completing his masterpiece.

David Fincher’s neo-noir follows veteran detective Somerset (Morgan Freeman) and hot-headed rookie Mills (Brad Pitt) as they hunt a killer staging murders around the seven deadly sins. In the final act, Doe turns himself in and leads the detectives to the desert. A delivery arrives with a box containing the head of Mills’ pregnant wife, Tracy. Consumed by “wrath,” Mills shoots Doe, becoming the final sin and completing the killer’s plan.

What makes the ending so effective is its structure. Doe has no need to escape because his plan includes his own death. The film also took an unusual approach to its marketing and credits, with Spacey’s name appearing only at the end rather than in the opening titles. Accounts of the production say that the filmmakers had to defend the bleak ending, and the line “What’s in the box?” became shorthand for the dread of a twist that cannot be undone.

2. The Usual Suspects (1995)

The villain: Keyser Söze / Verbal Kint (Kevin Spacey)
The victory: Fooling the police and vanishing.

Investigator Dave Kujan interrogates Roger “Verbal” Kint, a meek, disabled small-time crook who survived a deadly shootout on a ship. Kint describes a mythical crime lord, Keyser Söze, who supposedly orchestrated it all. Moments after Kint is released, Kujan realizes the story was assembled from details on his office bulletin board. Outside, Kint’s limp disappears, he lights a cigarette, climbs into a waiting car, and is gone.

Directed by Bryan Singer, the film earned Academy Awards for Christopher McQuarrie’s original screenplay and for Spacey in a supporting role. Its power comes from the way it turns the audience into the detective’s accomplice. Viewers believe Kint for the same reason Kujan does, because the story is vivid and the storyteller seems harmless. One of the film’s best-known lines, about the devil convincing the world he does not exist, describes the trick the movie itself performs.

3. Primal Fear (1996)

The villain: Aaron Stampler / “Roy” (Edward Norton)
The victory: Fooling his own lawyer and avoiding prison.

Defense attorney Martin Vail (Richard Gere) defends Aaron Stampler, an altar boy accused of murdering an archbishop. Vail uncovers what appears to be a violent alter ego, “Roy,” and wins a verdict of not guilty by reason of insanity. In the final scene, Aaron reveals there was no Roy at all. He faked the entire thing, got away with murder, and left Vail stunned.

The film was Edward Norton’s screen debut, and it earned him an Oscar nomination and a Golden Globe. Directed by Gregory Hoblit and adapted from a novel by William Diehl, it works as a courtroom drama until the last minutes, when it becomes a story about the limits of a lawyer’s certainty. Vail believed he was saving a damaged young man, and his victory in court is exactly what the villain wanted.

4. Gone Girl (2014)

The villain: Amy Dunne (Rosamund Pike)
The victory: Trapping her husband in a hollow marriage.

When Amy disappears, her husband Nick (Ben Affleck) becomes the prime suspect. In truth, she staged the whole thing as revenge for his infidelity. After killing her ex-boyfriend Desi and blaming him for her “abduction,” she returns home covered in blood, playing the victim. She then uses a pregnancy to keep Nick bound to her, leaving him trapped in a marriage with someone he knows is dangerous.

Gillian Flynn adapted her own novel for Fincher’s film, and Pike earned an Oscar nomination for the role. The story is told partly through Amy’s diary entries, which turn out to be as staged as the crime scene. Its most discussed moment is her “Cool Girl” monologue, in which she explains the roles she has played to please others. The ending is disturbing because Amy does not escape punishment by running away. She wins by returning to the center of the story and forcing everyone to accept her version of it.

Forces of Nature: Villains Who Cannot Be Stopped

Some villains do not need a plan that the audience can follow. They arrive like weather, and the story is about the people who stand in the way.

5. No Country for Old Men (2007)

The villain: Anton Chigurh (Javier Bardem)
The victory: Getting what he came for and walking away.

The Coen brothers’ adaptation of Cormac McCarthy’s novel gives us one of cinema’s most frightening forces of nature: a hitman armed with a cattle gun and a coin. After hunter Llewelyn Moss takes a briefcase of cash from a drug deal gone wrong, Chigurh tracks him relentlessly. Moss is killed off-screen by others, but Chigurh still collects the money and, as the film strongly implies, visits Moss’s widow, Carla Jean. He is badly hurt in a car crash, yet he limps away, unpunished.

The film won four Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Supporting Actor for Bardem. It denies viewers the conventional showdown they expect, and the choice is deliberate. Sheriff Ed Tom Bell, played by Tommy Lee Jones, spends the story one step behind Chigurh and eventually retires, overwhelmed by a kind of violence he cannot understand. The famous coin-toss scene in a gas station shows why the character is so unsettling: he treats a stranger’s life as a matter of chance he happens to control.

6. Avengers: Infinity War (2018)

The villain: Thanos (Josh Brolin)
The victory: Wiping out half of all life.

Marvel spent a decade and roughly 18 films building to Thanos, and he delivered. Most comic book villains are stopped at the last moment, but Thanos collects all six Infinity Stones and snaps his fingers. Half the universe, including many beloved heroes, turns to dust, and Thanos watches a sunrise from a quiet planet. The victory did not last forever, since Avengers: Endgame followed, but in this film he truly won.

Directed by Anthony and Joe Russo, the film does something rare for a blockbuster by giving its villain the emotional arc. Thanos sacrifices someone he loves to gain the Soul Stone and believes he is doing a terrible but necessary good. That conviction makes him more than a collection of powers. Audiences who left theaters in 2018 were stunned partly because the studio had trained them to expect the heroes to recover, and for once the ending refused to blink.

Systems That Reward the Wrong People

In the next two films, the villain does not need to be powerful in a physical sense. The world itself is arranged to protect him, and the hero’s sense of justice is the thing that loses.

7. Chinatown (1974)

The villain: Noah Cross (John Huston)
The victory: Keeping his wealth and power and getting away with it all.

In Roman Polanski’s classic noir, private investigator Jake Gittes (Jack Nicholson) is drawn into a web of corruption, water rights, and abuse in 1930s Los Angeles. At the climax, he tries to help Evelyn Mulwray escape her powerful father, Noah Cross. The police shoot Evelyn dead, and Cross takes custody of her daughter, who is also his own daughter. A shattered Gittes is led away with one of film’s most famous parting lines: “Forget it, Jake. It’s Chinatown.”

Robert Towne’s screenplay won an Oscar, and its water-scandal plot echoes real disputes over the water supply of early twentieth-century Los Angeles. Accounts of the production say that Towne wanted a gentler ending and that Polanski insisted on the tragic one. The result is a film that treats corruption as a permanent condition rather than a puzzle to be solved. Gittes is competent, determined and still unable to change the outcome, and that helplessness is the point.

8. Nightcrawler (2014)

The villain: Louis Bloom (Jake Gyllenhaal)
The victory: Building a business on exploitation and death.

This sharp satire of modern media follows Lou Bloom, an unhinged freelance crime videographer. To get the most shocking footage, he tampers with crime scenes and, at the climax, sends his own assistant into a deadly confrontation so that he can film it. Instead of being caught, Lou is rewarded: he gets paid, gains standing, and expands his company with a team of new interns.

Dan Gilroy wrote and directed the film as his feature debut, and Gyllenhaal reportedly lost a significant amount of weight to give Lou a hungry, wiry look. Rene Russo plays the news director who buys his footage, and Riz Ahmed plays the assistant, Rick. The most chilling part of the story is how ordinary Lou’s success looks. He speaks in the language of business seminars, and every employer, client and institution around him rewards him for it.

Wins That Come Through Sequels and Franchises

Franchise films complicate the idea of a victory. A villain may triumph in one installment, and the audience may know that the story will continue. These last two entries show different ways of handling that tension.

9. Star Wars: Episode V – The Empire Strikes Back (1980)

The villain: Darth Vader and the Empire
The victory: Crushing the Rebel Alliance.

Blockbuster sequels rarely end on a downer, but this one did. The Empire drives the rebels from Hoth, Han Solo is frozen in carbonite and handed to a bounty hunter, and Luke loses his hand in a lightsaber duel with Vader, who reveals he is Luke’s father. The heroes survive and regroup, so this is a looser kind of villain victory than the others on this list. Still, the film ends with the good guys beaten, scattered, and shaken.

Directed by Irvin Kershner, the film was written by Leigh Brackett and Lawrence Kasdan from a story by George Lucas. It broke from the tidy structure of the first movie, and many fans consider it the strongest of the series because it dares to leave the audience unresolved. The last scene, with the surviving heroes looking out at the stars while a rescue mission is planned, shows how a defeat can still push a story forward.

10. Saw (2004)

The villain: Jigsaw / John Kramer (Tobin Bell)
The victory: Trapping his victim and walking away.

This low-budget horror hit, which launched a huge franchise, opens with two men chained in a filthy bathroom with a body lying between them. Forced to play Jigsaw’s games, Dr. Lawrence Gordon saws off his own foot to try to escape. Meanwhile, the supposedly dead man on the floor, who is Kramer himself, gets up and locks photographer Adam in the dark.

James Wan directed the film from a script by Leigh Whannell, and it was made for roughly a million dollars before earning over one hundred million worldwide. Its closing reveal depends on the audience having stared at the same body for an hour without questioning it. The final words, delivered over a black screen, became a signature of the series. The ending also made Kramer a rare horror antagonist who never needs to chase his victims. He designs a situation and lets it work.

Why These Endings Linger

These films work because they break our expectation of justice. They leave us unsettled, and they stay with us long after the credits roll.

There is also a craft lesson in the pattern. A villain’s win only lands if the audience has spent the whole film believing the story is heading elsewhere. The Usual Suspects and Primal Fear rely on a reveal that recasts everything before it, while Chinatown and No Country for Old Men rely on a slow accumulation of dread. In both cases, the film earns its bleakness by making the hero’s effort feel real. The more a character fights, the more painful it is when the fight does not matter.

These movies also reveal what audiences are willing to accept. Studios have often worried that a downbeat ending will hurt word of mouth, yet several of the films above were hits, and some won major awards. The lesson many filmmakers took away is that audiences do not mind losing, so long as the loss feels honest to the story.

Honorable Mentions

Several other films belong in the same conversation. Rosemary’s Baby (1968) ends with a cult’s plan succeeding and its heroine accepting the outcome. The Wicker Man (1973) leaves its policeman at the mercy of islanders who set him up from the start. Fallen (1998) closes with a demon surviving to claim another victim, and Hereditary (2018) ends with a cult achieving the goal it has pursued throughout the story.
