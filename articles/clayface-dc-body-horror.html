Clayface: How DC Is Using Body Horror to Reshape the Comic Book Movie

By Maccarthy Albin

Published October 8, 2026 · Updated November 1, 2026

A lean, R-rated monster story was DC's most unusual bet yet. After a Halloween-season debut, here is how it was built and where it leaves the studio.

DC Studios' Clayface did something its R rating and its little-known title character suggested it might not: it finished its first weekend in first place. Directed by James Watkins from a screenplay by Mike Flanagan and Hossein Amini, the film opened in North American theaters on October 23, 2026, and collected an estimated $24 million domestically and about $19 million overseas, for a global start of roughly $43 million.

The numbers will not rattle the record books, but they matter for a film that cost around $44 million to make and that asks audiences to follow a disfigured actor rather than a caped hero. Tom Rhys Harries plays Matt Hagen, a rising Hollywood performer whose life collapses after an injury leaves him scarred. In desperation he submits to an experimental treatment, and the cure turns his body into living, shapeshifting clay while his mind and his sense of self begin to come apart.

A modest opening that beat expectations

Warner Bros. released Clayface in 3,458 North American theaters, where it narrowly edged out the A24 documentary You Can See Everything, which took in about $19.5 million in its second weekend. Pre-release forecasts had put the film's debut anywhere from the low $20 millions to the low $30 millions, and some trade coverage in the final week before opening suggested the documentary might even steal the top spot. That did not happen.

Audiences were a large part of the reason. Moviegoers polled by CinemaScore gave the film an A-, a strong grade for a horror title and the same mark earned by The Batman and Superman. Critical reaction was largely favorable, though not unanimous, with some reviewers praising the film's commitment to its grim tone and others finding its second half less assured than its first.

Context helps explain why a $24 million weekend can be read as a success. Recent DC releases such as Blue Beetle, which opened to about $25 million, and Aquaman and the Lost Kingdom, which opened to roughly $27.7 million, started in a similar range. Both were far more expensive productions. Clayface followed what industry observers describe as a Blumhouse-style model, keeping costs low enough that a respectable opening and decent word of mouth can carry the film into profit.

Inside the story of Matt Hagen

The film is built around a simple, cruel idea: a man whose face is his livelihood loses it. Hagen is an actor at the point where a career can tip toward stardom or oblivion, and the injury that disfigures him threatens to end everything at once. The treatment he turns to is overseen by a scientist played by Naomi Ackie, and it is the kind of promise that should make any viewer uneasy from the first scene.

What follows is less a superhero origin than a tragedy told through the language of horror. Hagen's body no longer obeys him. His features shift and sag, his limbs lose their shape, and the physical agony is matched by a growing fear that whatever is happening to his flesh is also happening to his identity. The film treats the clay as a metaphor for the unstable relationship between performance and self, which is a natural fit for a story about an actor who has spent his career wearing other people's faces.

That emphasis on private suffering is deliberate. Rather than building toward a citywide battle, the story stays close to Hagen, to the few people who see what he is becoming, and to the moral compromises made by those who stand to profit from his transformation.

The filmmakers behind the clay

The creative team reads like a deliberate collection of genre specialists. Watkins was hired to direct in February 2025, and Amini was already reworking the script by May. Watkins broke through with the 2008 horror-thriller Eden Lake, which won Best Horror Film at the Empire Awards, and he later directed The Woman in Black and the 2024 English-language remake of Speak No Evil. His strength has always been squeezing dread out of ordinary people in bad situations, a skill the Hagen story demands.

Flanagan, whose credits include Doctor Sleep, The Haunting of Hill House and Midnight Mass, conceived the story and wrote the original screenplay. He had been pitching a horror-minded take on the character for years and has pointed to the 1992 Batman: The Animated Series episode "Feat of Clay" as a formative influence. Matt Reeves, who directed The Batman, is among the producers, alongside James Gunn, Peter Safran and Lynn Harris.

The craft team adds weight. Rob Hardy is the cinematographer, Jon Harris the editor, and Volker Bertelmann, an Oscar winner for his score to All Quiet on the Western Front, composed the music. A film like this lives or dies on texture and sound, and the choices made in those departments are central to how the transformation scenes land.

The cast

Harries, a Welsh actor who was a relative newcomer to major studio films, was announced in the title role in June 2025. Gunn said at the time that he and Reeves had been won over after a long and exhaustive search, one that reportedly considered several established British performers. It is a big role for an actor to carry, because Hagen is on screen in some form for nearly the entire film and must convey both vanity and terror through a body that is constantly changing.

Ackie, who earned wide attention for playing the title role in Whitney Houston: I Wanna Dance with Somebody, anchors the film's scientific storyline. Max Minghella, Eddie Marsan, David Dencik, Nancy Carroll and Joshua James round out a supporting ensemble that gives the story a believable world of doctors, investigators and hangers-on around its central figure.

Four decades of mud: the comic book roots

Clayface is not one character but a mantle that has passed through several hands. The original, Basil Karlo, debuted in Detective Comics in 1940 as a horror actor who turned to crime after learning that a remake of one of his old films was in the works. The best-known later version, Matt Hagen, arrived in the early 1960s and, in the comics, was a treasure hunter who was exposed to a strange protoplasmic substance in a cave. Preston Payne, introduced in 1978, brought a sadder and more clinical take, a scientist whose touch was deadly to anyone he tried to hold.

It was the animated series that reshaped Hagen into the figure most fans now picture. In "Feat of Clay," voiced by Ron Perlman, Hagen is an actor who becomes dependent on a compound called Renuyu to restore his ruined face, and the episode's emotional power comes from watching a man mistake a quick fix for a cure. The film borrows that sensibility rather than copying the plot, keeping the actor's vanity, the desperate medical gamble and the slow loss of self that made the animated version so memorable.

The shapeshifting abilities that define the character in print, including the power to imitate other people and reshape his limbs into weapons, are present here, but they are filtered through pain. Where a conventional superhero film would treat such powers as spectacle, Clayface presents them as symptoms.

Why body horror, and why now

Body horror has a long and respected lineage in cinema, from David Cronenberg's The Fly to John Carpenter's The Thing and, more recently, Coralie Fargeat's The Substance. These films share a premise that bodies are fragile, that ambition and vanity invite punishment, and that the most frightening monsters are the ones we become. Clayface, a villain defined by a body that will not hold still, has always belonged to that tradition. It simply took a long time for a major studio to say so out loud.

For DC, the timing makes strategic sense. Under Gunn and Safran, the rebooted DC Universe has been presented as a collection of different kinds of stories rather than a single house style. Clayface is the third film in the new continuity, following Superman and Supergirl, and it is the studio's first true venture into horror and reportedly its first R-rated release in the current slate. Gunn has said repeatedly that not every DC film will look or sound like Superman, and this film is the clearest proof so far.

The economics of a smaller swing

Superhero filmmaking has spent the past decade ballooning in cost, which means the genre now needs enormous global grosses to look healthy. Supergirl, released earlier this year, carried a far larger price tag than Clayface and faced a much steeper climb to profitability. A film made for roughly $44 million operates under different rules. It can afford to be strange, to rely on practical effects and tight locations, and to limit its audience with an R rating without panicking about the consequences.

That freedom is visible on screen. The film is compact, its story is contained, and its scale is measured in rooms and hallways rather than skylines. Whether or not it becomes a long-term earner, it shows that DC can use its characters to make films at several price points, a flexibility that Marvel has found harder to maintain in recent years.

What it means for the DC Universe

Clayface is presented as a self-contained story, but it is an official entry in the DC Universe, and that leaves open the possibility of the character crossing paths with other figures from Gotham in the future. The studio has made no secret of its interest in the Batman corner of its world, and a villain who can imitate anyone is an obvious asset for any detective story. For now, the more immediate question is how the film's reception shapes the studio's appetite for other genre experiments, particularly lower-budget projects aimed at devoted audiences instead of the broadest possible crowd.

The film's second weekend arrives alongside Halloween, a favorable calendar position for a horror release, and strong audience scores suggest it has a chance to hold up better than many comic book openers do. Horror films often decline less sharply than big-budget franchise entries when word of mouth is good, so the next few weeks will say a lot about whether the early excitement was a spike or the start of a longer run.

Seeing it for yourself

Clayface opened in the United States and Canada on Friday, October 23, 2026, and began its international rollout a few days earlier, reaching France on October 21 and the United Arab Emirates on October 22. Tickets are available through major platforms such as Fandango, Atom Tickets and AMC Theatres, or directly from local cinemas. The film is rated R, and its transformation sequences are graphic enough that parents and more squeamish viewers should check content descriptions before buying a ticket.

Studios typically announce digital and streaming plans as a theatrical run winds down, so anyone hoping to wait for home viewing should watch for an official announcement from Warner Bros. rather than relying on unofficial sites. In the meantime, the big screen, with its full-volume sound design and unforgiving close-ups, is where the film's makers intended it to be seen.
