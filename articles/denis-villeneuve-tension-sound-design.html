How Denis Villeneuve Builds Tension With Scale and Sound

By Maccarthy Albin

October 3, 2026

A look at how Denis Villeneuve uses vast spaces, silence, and sound design to create tension in Sicario, Arrival, Blade Runner 2049, and Dune.

Few modern directors can make an empty room feel threatening the way Denis Villeneuve can. Across Prisoners, Sicario, Arrival, Blade Runner 2049, and the Dune films, he relies on a recognizable set of tools: enormous scale, patient pacing, and sound that works on the body as much as the ears.

The Quebec-born filmmaker rose through Canadian cinema with films such as Incendies before moving into large-scale Hollywood productions, yet his approach to suspense has barely changed. Whether the setting is a rain-soaked suburb or a desert planet, he treats tension as something to be built slowly and felt physically. Understanding how he does it means looking at three things working together: what we see, what we hear, and what we are made to wait for.

The held breath: how a Villeneuve scene begins

Watch the opening minutes of almost any of his thrillers and a pattern appears. The film does not announce danger with a loud noise or a rapid cut. Instead it establishes a space, lets the audience study it, and allows a feeling of unease to settle in before any threat is visible. Viewers often describe the experience as holding their breath without noticing when they started.

This is a deliberate reversal of the usual blockbuster formula, in which momentum is created by speed. Villeneuve creates momentum by pressure. Each shot adds weight to the one before it, and the lack of release becomes the engine of the scene. When something finally does happen, the audience has been primed to feel it far more intensely than a quicker film could manage.

Sicario: dread on an ordinary road

Sicario (2015) is perhaps the cleanest demonstration of the method. Emily Blunt plays an FBI agent drawn into a cross-border operation led by characters whose motives she cannot read. The film is shot by Roger Deakins and scored by Jóhann Jóhannsson, and the pairing of their work gives it an almost oppressive calm.

The convoy sequence at the border is a textbook case. Nothing in it is complicated: vehicles move through traffic in a busy city while the agents scan for threats. What makes it unbearable is the way the camera and the score refuse to tell us when to relax. The music is a slow, low pulse that seems to approach from far away, and the longer it plays without a payoff, the tighter the scene becomes. By the time the violence arrives, it feels less like a surprise than a release of pressure the audience has been carrying for minutes.

The night raid through a tunnel pushes the same idea further. Viewed partly through thermal and night-vision imagery, the soldiers become shapes moving through darkness. The film strips away detail rather than adding it, and in doing so makes the audience's imagination do the frightening work.

Arrival: when sound becomes language

In Arrival (2016), sound is not simply atmosphere. It is the subject. The story follows a linguist, played by Amy Adams, who is brought in to communicate with visitors whose ships have appeared around the world. Because the central problem is communication, the film has to make the audience feel what it is like to confront something that cannot be understood.

The sound work was recognized with an Academy Award for sound editing, credited to Sylvain Bellemare. The alien vocalizations are layered, resonant, and strange, designed to feel biological without resembling any familiar creature. Jóhannsson's score amplifies the effect with deep tones and unusual vocal textures, while the quiet interior of the ship becomes a place where every small noise matters. Max Richter's piece On the Nature of Daylight is used to open and close the story, offering a human counterpoint to the cold geometry of the visitors.

The tension here is not about physical danger so much as uncertainty. The audience is asked to listen as carefully as the characters do, and that act of listening is what produces the suspense.

Blade Runner 2049: a world with its own weather

Following up a cult classic is a risky assignment, and Blade Runner 2049 (2017) approaches it by amplifying the original's mood rather than copying its plot. Deakins won the Academy Award for cinematography for his work on the film, which fills the screen with monumental structures, drifting dust, and hazy light. A ruined Las Vegas bathed in orange becomes one of the most striking images in recent science fiction, and the effect is to make the protagonist, played by Ryan Gosling, look like a tiny figure drifting through a world that does not notice him.

The sound design follows suit. Benjamin Wallfisch and Hans Zimmer composed a score filled with deep, rumbling tones and echoing brass that nod to the sonic identity of the 1982 original without imitating it. Rather than steering emotion with melody, the music often acts as an environment, vibrating through the frame like heat. The result is a film where even quiet conversations carry an undercurrent of unease.

Dune: building a planet you can hear

With Dune (2021) and its sequel, Villeneuve applied his methods to a story that demands world-building at an enormous scale. The result won six Academy Awards at the 2022 ceremony, including Best Sound and Best Original Score, a sign of how central the audio design was to the film's identity.

Hans Zimmer took an unorthodox route, building the music from unusual instruments, human voices, and heavy percussion, often treating the orchestra as one part of a larger texture. The sound team, led by Mark Mangini and Theo Green on the first film, shaped the machinery, wind, and sand into a sonic landscape that feels physical. Ornithopters beat their wings with an insect-like thrum, and the sandworms announce themselves with a vibration long before they appear. Viewers in a well-equipped theater often report feeling the sound in their chests.

The sequel, Dune: Part Two (2024), extended this thinking with a sequence set on the home world of the Harkonnens, shot with infrared cinematography that turns the screen into a stark black-and-white world lit by a black sun. Visual starkness and a harsh, percussive soundscape combine to make the setting feel alien and cruel in a matter of seconds, with very little dialogue required.

Scale that makes people feel small

Across these films, Villeneuve often frames characters against structures or landscapes that dwarf them. The alien ship in Arrival hangs over a field like a monolith. The architecture of Dune is built for giants. These images communicate power and indifference without a line of dialogue. The audience senses that the characters are small compared with the forces around them, which raises the stakes before anything has happened.

The technique is as much psychological as visual. When a character is dwarfed by their surroundings, the viewer senses vulnerability instinctively. A wide frame that might serve as a simple establishing shot in another film becomes a statement: this person does not control what happens next.

Patience as a technique

His films tend to hold shots longer than most blockbusters do. That patience is not about slowness for its own sake. It lets anticipation build. A lingering shot invites the audience to scan the frame, to look for threats, and to become participants in the suspense. The longer the film withholds an answer, the more the viewer's mind supplies possibilities, and almost none of them are comfortable.

This is also why his editing rarely feels frantic even in the middle of action. The geography of a scene stays clear, the movement is easy to follow, and the dread comes from knowing exactly where everyone is and what might come through the next door.

Silence is part of the mix

Just as important is what he leaves out. Quiet stretches make the loud moments hit harder, and they force the audience to lean in. Because the sound is so carefully shaped, a small noise, like a door creaking or a distant rumble, can carry real menace.

Silence in his films is rarely empty. It is full of room tone, distant machinery, and breathing, all mixed with care. By lowering the baseline, the sound team makes every added element feel significant. A single low note arriving after a long quiet stretch can feel more violent than an entire orchestra.

The team behind the tension

It would be a mistake to credit one person alone. Villeneuve has built long working relationships that shape the consistency of his films. Deakins photographed Prisoners, Sicario, and Blade Runner 2049, while Greig Fraser handled the visual design of the Dune films. Jóhannsson shaped the sound of his earlier thrillers until his death in 2018, and Zimmer has become a defining voice in his later epics. Each collaborator brings craft, but the shared philosophy is clear: images and sound are meant to be designed together, not layered on after the fact.

How to watch for it yourself

The easiest way to appreciate the technique is to watch with intention. Choose a scene and try it three ways. First, watch with the sound off and notice how the framing and pacing alone create unease. Next, close your eyes and listen, paying attention to how the low frequencies and background textures shape your mood. Finally, watch normally and see how the two layers combine.

If possible, see these films on the largest screen and best sound system available. His work is designed for spaces where the audience can feel the vibration of the sound and the scale of the image. A small speaker can flatten the lowest tones, which are often where the tension lives.

Why it works

The common thread is trust in the audience. Villeneuve does not explain every emotion or fill every gap. He creates conditions in which viewers feel the tension themselves. That is why his films reward being seen in a theater with a strong sound system, and why they stay in the mind long after the plot details fade.

In an era when much of mainstream filmmaking leans on speed and spectacle, his work stands out for its confidence in restraint. A towering frame, a held shot, and a single sustained note can accomplish what pages of dialogue cannot. That is the lasting lesson of his style: the most powerful tension is not the kind a film announces, but the kind it allows you to discover on your own.
